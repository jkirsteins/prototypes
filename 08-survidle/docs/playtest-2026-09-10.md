## Addressed since the session

Read on 2026-09-11 against main and the branch `worktree-survidle-playtest-fixes`. Each finding below names the commit that answers it, so a reader knows which of the record's items still stand.

**The sleep legibility work** (`56942580`, `b3156171`, `c8f83501`, `fb1fe98d`, spec `docs/superpowers/specs/2026-09-10-survidle-sleep-legibility-design.md`) answers **bug 1** (75-76, 89, 91, 101-107, 114, 116). The mechanism: a stamina collapse was represented as a sleep and held until Stamina reached 100, while the bar floors at 99, so "too exhausted" at 99 was the collapse latch one point short of releasing, and each sleep was a finite task that could finish before the body's real need, so the scheduler started another one. Now a collapse is a Rest released at 55, sleep is decided by sleepiness alone, one sleep task runs to the live wake crossing instead of restarting, the stats carry "recovering from collapse, work resumes at N Stamina", and a Sleepiness bar reads "Wake about 07:10". The same work rewrote the light-sleeper card (7, 95-96) as "Light sleeper. Safe from wolves while asleep. Storms reduce sleep quality." and let a log be split in any weather, yielding wet firewood (52-56).

**Bug 2, the once field fire that kept lighting** (117-118, 133): **confirmed and fixed** on the branch. A probe on seed 30 with a drill and firewood lit ten field fires in five hours off one once order. `orderMet` in `src/sim/orders.ts` judged every light order by the region's camp fire, and a field fire lives on the survivor, not the region, so a once field fire was never met and ran again each time the last had burned down. A once or a counted light now reads its tally; a keep still reads the fire. The regression test is `tests/playtest-2026-09-10.test.ts`.

**Bug 3, the camp that was and was not there** (22, 89-90, 106, 109, 111, 142-147): **three faults, all fixed** on the branch, and one thing that was right. The map tooltip named a cell off the world's generated spot list, which carries a "camp" spot for every region as the ground it offers for one, so the landing read "Camp" with no camp made; it now reads its ground until somebody makes camp there. The Do list offered a walk row to that same spot, reading "Walk to ?"; it no longer does. The body said "no way to camp" whether there was no route or no camp; with no camp it now says "no camp yet", on the row and in the log. What was right: the goal "Choose where to live" counts the deed of making camp, and no camp had been made. The observer's withdrawn claim at 145 was half right - the tester did not have a camp - and the UI was what said otherwise.

**Bug 4, the ice hole** (121-122): **not a bug.** Thirst care cuts an ice hole when the shore is iced over (`src/sim/body.ts`, `iceHoleSite`), and seed 30 lands on ice. What was missing was the why: the activity strip read "Self-care" over "opening an ice hole". It now reads "Self-care: thirsty", and every care row names its need the same way (sleepy, cold, hungry, exhausted, storm coming, going home; camp maintenance says "the fire" or "the snares"). That is the cheap half of proposed improvement 2; the cancel or wake control is a design question and untouched.

**Smaller items fixed on the branch:** the Gear tab scrolls (84) - the pane clipped its content at the tab box's height with no scroll of its own, and at the landing the list is already taller than the box; the Journal's next-season line says "expected in about 3 months" rather than "105 days" (77); and the task bar's seconds count read the hurry's rate curve since `135e21e1`, which is the "faster, then slower" half of 72-74.

## Summary

### 1. What happened

One blind run lasted about 16 minutes, followed by a debrief. The tester was intrigued by the opening and immediately wanted to pursue hunting and questing after seeing animals. He sought stone as part of that plan, while also trying to make fire and tools. He never completed "Choose where to live." The trip for stone became the turning point: movement, self-care, sleep, repeated field fires, and an ice-hole action appeared to happen outside his control. He did not die, but stopped meaningfully pursuing his own plans. His debrief captured the arc: "i liked the beginning - really intriguing" (126), then "when my character started doing things by itself, it became a thoroguhly unpleasant experience" (131, 155).

The observer intervened once during play by pointing out the action filter (46). Three further interventions happened only during the debrief: an incorrect claim that no camp existed, later withdrawn (142, 145); an explanation that the game was intended as idle (148); and a correction that it was not intended as survival + roguelike (150).

### 2. What is working

- The opening creates curiosity and possibility. The tester liked being presented with a place, skills, and things to do, and described the beginning as "really intriguing" (126). He later framed it as "a lot of toys" (130).
- The atmosphere landed strongly. He liked the sound effects during play (10), praised the weather widget twice without prompting (25-26), and said in debrief that the audio created a great feeling, the ambience was great, and he felt immersed (127, 129).
- The live world produced delight. "omg this is real time, everything is moving" was an unprompted positive realization (24). Animals immediately produced a concrete hunting and questing plan, urgency, and a resource-gathering chain (28-29, 155).
- The presentation and crafting combination has appeal for this tester. He likes survival crafting games and called the roguelike presentation plus crafting a good combination (138-139).
- Clear goals and progression verbs are satisfying. He reacted positively when a new goal appeared (13), and later singled out "gather more resources" and "build a basic camp" as satisfying, comparing the desired arc to Minecraft's first shelter and later evolution (136-137).
- Several interface elements worked on first contact: mouseover (15), the Tip (12), Settings (99), and especially the Log, which he called great after finding it (85-86).
- The failure was memorable rather than merely dull. The tester called the sleeping episode "incredibly funny" even though it also drove the strongest frustration (135). That does not reduce the severity of the control problem, but it shows the simulation can generate stories.

### 3. Findings, ordered by weight

#### Bugs or likely bugs

1. The action and self-care loop appears internally contradictory and became the session's blocker. The character repeatedly refused a queued walk as "too exhausted" while Energy showed 99 or 100, restarted sleep, emitted "no way to camp," and still made partial travel progress (75-76, 89, 91, 101-107, 114, 116). The tester repeatedly tried to understand or interrupt it and eventually described the experience as a loss of control (131-134). This needs reproduction before deciding which parts are intended automation.
2. A "once" field-fire action appeared to execute repeatedly. The tester noticed multiple fires, and the observer confirmed that the queued action was marked "once" (117-118). The repeated fires became part of the debrief's loss-of-control account (133).
3. Camp identity and goal state disagreed. The starting place reported "Camp" on mouseover, supporting the tester's belief that he had a camp, while "Choose where to live" remained incomplete and the game emitted "no way to camp" (22, 89-90, 106, 109, 111, 142-147). The observer initially blamed the tester, then explicitly withdrew that reading and identified a regression involving original camps (142, 145).
4. An ice-hole action appeared while the tester was trying to make a stone knife, with no understood reason (119-122). It may be another queue-selection or repeat bug, but the record does not establish the trigger.

#### Legibility failures

1. The player could not tell why the character was acting. Unexpected travel to bare rock, automatic self-care, sleep restarts, meals, repeated fires, and the ice-hole action all lacked a visible causal chain the tester could follow (60-65, 68-76, 82-83, 89, 95-103, 117-122). The Log exposed outcomes but did not restore control or explain the governing rule (85-86, 104-105).
2. The queue model required repeated rediscovery. "up" and "down" first read as improvement controls, not priority controls; a large queued batch then caused a second realization that it was a queue (27, 33-34, 49-51). Later, queued work did not appear to displace self-care (92, 101).
3. The opening inventory and camp state were not legible. The tester saw meat but did not initially understand that he also carried an axe, and he believed the starting location was an existing camp (22-23, 40-41, 126, 142-147).
4. Status values showed level but not motion or cause. The tester could not tell whether warmth was falling and proposed direction indicators himself (82, 115). Maximum Energy appearing alongside "too exhausted" made the status display actively contradict the action log (91, 103-104, 116).
5. Action constraints read as arbitrary. Splitting a log appeared blocked by "dry weather" while the widget said overcast and Wet was zero, making the earlier tree-felling action feel pointless (52-56). The precise reason remains unverified.
6. Several smaller controls or labels were unclear: the "2" beside field fire (66), the temporary nature of speed-up (67, 71-74), the Eat button and lean-meat limit (78-79, 82), Journal content that looked clickable (93-94), and inaccessible lower Gear content (84).
7. Long-range timing was presented with false-feeling precision. The tester laughed at "berries expected in 105 days" and preferred a human-scale duration such as three months (77).

#### Things the tester was never told

1. Keyboard movement was absent despite an immediate expectation of WASD or arrow-key control (16-17).
2. Clicking locations and choosing activities had an unclear relationship. The tester repeatedly believed he needed to travel directly to animals, camp, trees, or resources, then was surprised when travel occurred (31-32, 37-39, 62-65).
3. The action filter was not discovered unaided. It only became useful after intervention 1, after which the tester found cordage and resumed planning (45-48).
4. Traits and flavor text did not communicate mechanical consequences. "Mighty," "Sleeps light," "Wolves never reach the bed," and the storm-night description all caused questions or confusion (3, 6-8). "Sleeps light" later appeared to promise behavior opposite to what the tester experienced (95-96).
5. "Lee ground" was unexplained (20).
6. The skills system looked substantial but was never meaningfully investigated. The tester called it both impressive and overwhelming and explicitly limited the confidence of his own assessment (11, 140).
7. The route from seeing an animal to hunting it was unclear. The tester went to the animal, inferred that Hunting 1 versus 4 blocked him, decided he needed a bow, and pursued stone to advance that plan, but never found a confirmed hunting action (28-36, 155).

#### Design questions

1. What control contract should the game promise? The tester enjoyed the survival-crafting and roguelike framing but strongly rejected unexplained autonomous actions (126-139). After being told it was an idle game, he compared it to Dwarf Fortress and RimWorld and pointed out that indirect control does not itself make a game idle (148-153). The design needs to decide whether the player directs intent, queues exact actions, or manages an autonomous survivor.
2. Should the first session narrow itself around shelter, warmth, and one tool chain? The tester found the breadth intriguing and overwhelming at the same time, asked to hide tree choices, and lost the original goal while searching many actions (18, 21, 38-45, 50, 59, 126-130, 136-137).
3. Should a visible camp be a location label, a built structure, or both? The current mixed meaning undermined the first goal and every later "go home" decision (22, 89-90, 106, 109, 111, 142-147).
4. How much of the weather simulation should be foregrounded? The widget was loved (25-26), but the map weather later went unnoticed, sunlight was mistaken for field-fire illumination, and two areas appeared unaffected by sunlight (87, 108, 110, 112-113).

### 4. Proposed improvements

Cheapest and highest-leverage changes first:

1. Fix the reproducible state contradictions before tuning onboarding: camp identity versus goal state, Energy 99-100 versus "too exhausted," "once" actions repeating, sleep restarting, and unexpected ice-hole selection (91, 102-105, 117-122, 142-147).
2. Put the cause of every automatic substitution beside the active activity. A single line should state what the survivor is doing, why the requested action is blocked, what threshold must change, and whether progress is still occurring. Give the player an explicit cancel or wake control if interruption is intended to be possible (68-76, 83, 95-105).
3. Replace ambiguous queue controls with explicit priority language such as "earlier" and "later," and show which action is active, queued, blocked, or automatic. Ensure "once" visibly leaves the queue after completion (27, 33-34, 49-51, 101, 117-118).
4. Make the first goal and world agree. If a starter camp exists, recognize it and describe the next upgrade. If it does not, do not label the location Camp or emit "no way to camp"; guide the player through choosing and building one (106, 111, 136-147).
5. Add the tester's unprompted status-trend idea: compact rising, falling, or stable indicators on the survival bars, plus a short reason on hover (115). This could also expose why self-care overrides work.
6. Use progressive disclosure for the first survival chain. Keep a small set of goal-relevant actions visible, hide tree-species choice until it changes an outcome, and surface the filter before presenting the full catalog (38-51, 59, 128-130).
7. Show carried gear and consumables as one coherent inventory summary. The axe should not be discoverable only after the player already believes they carry meat alone (23, 40-41).
8. Rewrite condition messages in terms of the actual blocking variable. If snow, precipitation, material wetness, daylight, or location blocks log splitting, name that state rather than the broader phrase "dry weather" (52-56).
9. Add keyboard navigation or clearly establish map-click navigation immediately. The tester requested WASD or arrows before understanding the rest of the control model (16-17).
10. Present long forecasts approximately, for example "about 3 months," while retaining exact timing in a tooltip if useful (77).
11. When an animal sighting creates hunting intent, expose one clear next step and its dependency chain. Distinguish "travel to inspect," "hunt here," insufficient skill, and missing gear without making the player infer the route through unrelated action groups (28-36, 155).

### 5. Roadmap followups

- Define and test the game's control philosophy: direct survivor control, intent-level orders, or autonomous simulation management. The answer should govern queue behavior, self-care, interruption, feedback, and marketing language together (131-154).
- Prototype a shelter-first first-night arc with a clear camp upgrade path, then compare it against the current open action catalog. Preserve the sense of many future possibilities while limiting immediate choices (126-130, 136-139).
- Explore the survival + roguelike direction as a deliberate product question, not as a conclusion from one tester. The tester liked that combination, while the author stated it was not the original intention (139, 150-151).
- Revisit map lighting and weather readability together. Preserve the praised weather widget and atmosphere, but distinguish sunlight, firelight, cloud shadow, and unlit regions without adding visual clutter (25-26, 87, 108, 110, 112-113).
- Reconsider the 50 m zoom only after deciding what simulation detail exists at that scale. The observer found the current zoom ugly because cells were not properly simulated (123).
- Give short-lived wildlife enough readable persistence and hunting guidance that a sighting can become an informed quest rather than an uncertain flash or an improvised dependency search (28-36, 124, 155).

### 6. What to discard

- Discard the observer's initial claim that the tester simply had no camp and failed to understand it. The UI itself labeled the first place "Camp," and the observer withdrew the claim after recognizing a regression (90, 142-145). Keep the camp-state bug and communication failure.
- Do not treat the temporary speed boost as a bug based on this session. The tester initially thought speed changed unpredictably, then recognized that the boost was temporary (72-73). Its discoverability and usefulness remain fair questions (71, 74).
- Do not conclude that the field fire brightened the whole map. The tester made that inference, but the observer identified sunlight as the cause (110, 112-113). Keep the lighting distinction problem, not the causal claim.
- Do not rebalance stone gathering because the tester called it his "biggest mistake." His final comment explains that stone was a rational step in his hunting and questing plan; the trip became unpleasant because movement and autonomy were unclear (60-65, 88, 131-132, 155).
- Do not simplify or remove the skills system based on this test. The tester barely engaged with it and said so directly (11, 140).
- Do not act on the unresolved aside attached to min-maxing. Its attribution was never clarified (5).
- Do not infer that Hunting 1 versus 4 actually prevents hunting. The tester guessed this and acted on that belief, but never completed a hunt or confirmed the rule (29-36, 155).
